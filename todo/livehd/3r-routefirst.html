3r-routefirst Native routing with movable logic

Route and legalize in one loop, guided by routing instead of placement, and be better and faster than single-thread OpenROAD. Run the router with lhd route. It routes every signal net on M2 to M7, moves the cells that block a pin, and with --set route.prelegal=true gives each cell its site when the first net that needs it is routed.

Rewritten 2026-11-24. The router exists and is measured; this page keeps what is still to do. The method, the rule table and the measurements are in tools/routefirst/README.md. The first plan (PCRS and flops frozen, OpenROAD as data router, subtasks A to G) is finished and superseded; its page is in the git history up to commit 4d31a8b7e. The synthesis DEF export feeds lhd route directly.

Depends on

  • 2r-route supplies the pose before terminal legalization and the pin slacks (legroute_plan*/input.def, timing.tsv).
  • 2p-pcrs supplies the clock tree. Its cells are in the DEF; its wires are not (see Clock).
  • 2f-scalability supplies the chunks for routing by chunks.
  • 3s-postroute is the finishing pass after this router (free M2 and M3 for power and clock, fillers, antennas). Not started.

Direction (user, 2026-11-24)

  • The flow must not call OpenROAD. OpenROAD is the referee only: design rules, extraction and timing of a finished result.
  • Flops may move during routing, with their clock leaves rerouted or the movement of clock cells limited.
  • The most critical or most complex nets choose first. Cells that do not fit move inside their tile, then to a nearby tile with the lowest demand.
  • Long, very critical nets may get extra spacing. The spare M2 and M3 go to the finishing pass.

Where it stands

  • Small designs (minimax, sha3, ternip; 15k to 21k nets). Every net routed, no rule or connectivity finding, 34 to 80 CPU-seconds against 1,247 to 2,179 for single-thread OpenROAD, and 9 to 28% higher Fmax. Route-first from the pose before legalization uses 3 to 11% less wire than routing the legalized placement.
  • Density. No advantage: rf_route closes what OpenROAD closes up to the placer's limit on sha3 and ternip, and OpenROAD closes one minimax point (0.88) where route-first leaves 9 nets.
  • Eyeriss (1,419 µm die, 1,040 macros, 294k to 438k nets). Fully routed on the LiveHD frame and on the ORFS-placement frame, 15 to 51 times less CPU than OpenROAD. The ORFS netlist placed by LiveHD stops with 52 of 433,445 nets left.

Clock

PCRS builds and places the clock tree, and the DEF LiveHD writes after final legalization carries its 4,170 clock nets (eyeriss) tagged as clock but without wires. rf_route routes them itself, all of them before the first data net, with the ordinary rules. The frames of the first experiment were different: there the clock and reset wires came fixed with the frame and the router only saw data nets.

Still to do

  1. Into the flow. rf_route is a separate program that reads DEF and LEF and has the ASAP7 rule limits compiled in. The flow still ends at placement. Move it into pass/place on ids, without name-keyed copies of what the graph holds, with the rules taken from the technology (widths, vias, the four measured limits per layer), the slacks from the fast timer and the tile demand from Global_routes. A second technology (sky130) is the test that nothing is ASAP7 by accident.
  2. Who routes the clock. Decide between PCRS wires that the router must respect and rf_route routing the tree as it does now. If the router keeps it: a length cap or balance for leaf nets, spacing for trunks, and skew measured after routing and after a flop moved. Today flops move at most 2 sites and 0 to 7 of them do per small design, so that freedom is neither a measured gain nor a measured risk.
  3. The tail. On the ORFS-netlist eyeriss frame 25 nets fail at a pin walled in by other nets after the rip-ups allowed, and 27 more are not reached in 6,000 s. Failed calls are 14% of the routing time on that frame (1% on the LiveHD frame, 7% on the ORFS placement). Rip-up puts the same wires back; the walled pin needs its landing kept, as macro pins now have, or the blockers need a cost for coming back.
  4. Tree order and spacing per net. One setting does not fit: alpha 0.3 is best on the small designs, 0.6 gives 39% on the LiveHD eyeriss frame and nothing on the ORFS one, 0 and 1.0 are bad. Choose by the span of the net and the strength of its driver. Spacing the worst 2% gives 3 to 28%, but leaves 1 to 4 nets unrouted on eyeriss, which has to be fixed before it becomes a default.
  5. A share of the longest nets first (user, 2026-11-24). All longest first is not faster and leaves more nets. A share ahead of the rest (--long-frac) was measured on the LiveHD eyeriss frame: with the longest 5% first the run took 1,349 CPU-seconds against 1,567, with the same wire (4,604 mm), every net routed and 157.9 MHz against 161.4. So it saves 14% of the CPU and costs 2% of the frequency; the setting is neutral on timing and is not a default. The same runs on the other two frames were stopped before they finished.
  6. Search cost. The search advances one track stop per expansion, so a connection of a millimetre costs 28,000 expansions at best and a clock leaf net 2,800 per pin. Advance by runs of free track, and use the tile routes as corridors. The hash tables that made eyeriss fit cost about 15% on the small designs.
  7. Fresh slacks. Order, anchors and spacing use the placer's slacks from before routing. Their rank agrees with the routed slacks on minimax and ternip (0.98, 0.99) and less on sha3 (0.88). Refresh after a batch of nets, or only the cones of changed branches.
  8. A raw global placement as input. The pose used so far is nearly legal (148 to 280 overlapping pairs). Route-first legalization from an overlapping placement is untested, and so is the claim that it could close a denser core.
  9. Rules not served. Nondefault widths, shields with a power connection, and wide wires. Only the default width with double spacing is served.
  10. Long nets without buffers. LiveHD leaves nets of 2 to 3.6 mm behind one buffer on eyeriss, and the worst paths run through them in OpenROAD's route and in rf_route's. In the eyeriss run every buffer-repair sweep was refused by the hold guard (4 in the repair step and 15 in the three repair-loop iterations): hold slack would have fallen from −337 ps to −410 to −610 ps, against an allowance of 50 ps. Setup would have improved. Either the guard needs to trade hold against setup per path, or hold repair must come after routing (see 3s-postroute), or the sweep must buffer the long nets alone. This belongs to placement (2w-wlresynth and the virtual buffer trees); the router can only limit the damage.
  11. Checks in the repository. The rule limits were measured with OpenROAD on two-wire layouts by local scripts. Fixtures that pin each limit of the table, and the two-wire oracle itself, are not in the repository.

What this says. Spreading by pin density works (41 open nets against 706) and moderate spreading costs no frequency, but spreading the whole design does (8% on O). A lower density target does nothing. The placer's own pin inflation does nothing either, on any measurement grid: before legalization the pose has clumps of 90 to 500 pins per µm² with it and without it, because the placer's forces act on the 64 by 64 density grid and cannot shape what is inside a 5.5 µm bin. What the final placement shows is what the final legalizer enforces, and that legalizer gives its 10% most critical nets' cells (33,277 of 230,262 on O) no padding at all.

New, all off by default:

  • synth.place.legalize.pin_footprint=N: a cell is at least as wide, with its padding, as its signal pins need at N pins per µm². It depends on the cell alone. pin_padding_critical=true applies it to the critical cells too, pin_padding_max caps the extra sites per side, and a cell gives the extra up when it adds more than pin_padding_rows rows to its move.
  • synth.place.legalize.pin_density=N: extra padding only for cells whose input pose lies in a bin above N pins per µm². Weaker than the footprint: a cell pushed into a cool bin carries no padding.
  • route.pin_cost=K, route.pin_tile: M2 and M3 wire costs more over tiles with more pins than the limit, so wires that only pass go over them on M4 and above. No loss on C (all nets, 8% less CPU, 319.2 against 320.5 MHz); the runs on O are not finished.

The user's direction is to have pin density in global placement from the start, before any timing iteration, so that the flow adapts to the wire it costs. The footprint is the form of it that can be given to every step: the same number as a floor on the cell's charge in global placement from the first iteration, and as padding in each legalizer. The placer half needs a per-cell inflation floor in DREAMPlace and is not built. One sample points the same way: with the placer's fine pin grid on and the legalizer padding, O ends at 2.0% more wirelength and +128 ps of estimated setup slack, against 3.0% and −44 ps with the legalizer padding alone.

Routing by chunks

Asked by the user on 2026-11-24. Placement already runs in chunks with a coordinator that owns the boundary slots (2f-scalability). An experimental grid worker now runs independently on a shared LiveHD post-PCRS placement. In theory a design can be cut into many small chunks, each with its own routing: eyeriss has five or six today, and the number can be pushed up until it stops paying. What is unclear is the long wires, which cross chunks and often more than one. The slot-based methods below remain experiments to compare against the working grid implementation.

Native grid experiment. rf_chunks routes tile interiors in separate processes, reserves 128 DBU at seams and gives crossing nets and spills to the coordinator. It can also route the crossing nets first. The resident router reports failed pins to the agent. rf_feedback releases nearby wires, routes the failed nets first, and verifies the assembly. Four interior workers on ASAP7 NVDLA M retained 18,545 routes. The first assembly had nine opens. A 540 DBU repair released 231 nets and left one open; a 1,400 DBU repair released 95 and closed it. Extraction at the 1,500 ps constraint gave 1,141.81 MHz, versus 1,148.21 MHz flat and 1,108.20 MHz for four-thread OpenROAD on the same PCRS placement. M2–M7 routing, extraction and timing used identical LEFs and constraints. The workers currently load the whole input frame. The two-terminal boundary-slot experiment below exercises independent halves. Multi-terminal boundary trees and memory-bounded routing packets remain open.

The native router now runs inside lhd route. lhd route chunk consumes the same synthesis DEF bundle and optional placement regions, schedules separate workers using the running driver, and verifies the assembly. lhd route repair accepts the preceding checkpoint. The driver reference gives the public commands and socket protocol.

On NVDLA M, a second 4 by 2 run reused all eight workers. Repair closed the ten remaining nets, and the exported DEF passed lhd route verify. Its bytes matched the preceding standalone router on the same placement and settings. A separate run using the two floorplanner regions left thirteen opens after assembly. Pin-neighbor repair reduced those to one, and pin-access reservation closed the last net. Readback through lhd route verify reproduced the published checkpoint findings on all five NVDLA partitions. M, A and P were complete. O and C had 268 and 802 open nets respectively, with no other native geometry findings.

NVDLA routing evolution and retained experiment results

What chunks would buy

  • Wall time. rf_route is one thread. Eyeriss takes 26 to 100 minutes; chunks route in parallel.
  • The tail. Time grows faster than the net count because late nets search a full chip. A chunk bounds every search and every rip-up.
  • Memory per worker, and incremental work: a change inside a chunk reroutes that chunk if its boundary stays as it was.

What a boundary is for a track router

Wires run on tracks in one direction per layer. A wire can cross a vertical chunk edge only on a horizontal layer (M2, M4, M6) and a horizontal edge only on M3, M5 or M7. A boundary pin is therefore a slot: an edge, a layer and a track. If the chunks tile the die, the two sides of an edge share the slot and there is no top-level wire at all: a net that crosses is two half nets that end on the same slot. A net that passes through a chunk without a pin in it is a feedthrough, a two-slot net of that chunk like any other. Supply is not the limit: a vertical edge has about 26 M2, 21 M4 and 16 M6 tracks per micron.

The chunks must also agree on the rules at the edge, because the limits reach across it: 31 to 48 nm end to end on one track, facing ends on the next track, 34 nm between cuts. Either a contract (no wire end and no cut within a margin of the edge except at a slot) or an order (neighbours route one after the other and see each other's edge wires) is needed. The contract keeps chunks independent. The order needs four colours of chunks and makes a reroute depend on the neighbours.

Three ways to handle the long wires

  1. Long wires first. Route every net that crosses a chunk edge on the whole chip, freeze it, and hand each chunk those wires as fixed metal. The long nets get straight tracks and the best layers, and they are the critical ones: on eyeriss the worst paths are nets of millimetres. But this part is serial and sees the whole chip, a crossing net still ends at cell pins inside chunks, and a change to one such net touches several chunks. It is the "longest first" order under another name, and the flat test of that order (item 5 above) is its first data point.
  2. Slots first, chunks independent. The coordinator gives every crossing net its slots from the tile estimate, then each chunk routes alone and respects them. This scales, runs in parallel and keeps a reroute local. The cost is that a slot is chosen before any wire exists: the route that the flat router would take may want another track, another layer or another place along the edge, and the detour is paid on both sides.
  3. Slots that move. Start as in 2. Each chunk reports, for each slot, what moving it would save: tracks along the edge, or a layer. The coordinator moves a slot when the two sides together gain, and only the two half nets are rerouted. A net that failed in a chunk for lack of a way to its slot asks first. One or two rounds, critical nets first, which is the route-first idea applied to slots instead of sites.

My expectation: 2 is the base because it is the only one that scales, 3 recovers most of what 2 loses, and 1 stays for the few nets where a slot is the wrong abstraction: the clock trunks and the handful of unbuffered nets that cross the die. A mixed rule (the worst share by slack and span goes first on the upper layers, the rest gets slots) costs little serial time if the share is a few percent.

What needs care

  • One tree per net. The coordinator fixes which edges a net crosses, as a tree over the chunks. Each chunk then joins its own pins and slots of that net. Two crossings of one net between the same two chunks would make a loop, and a chunk that connects its slots but not to each other would leave the net open, so the tree must say which slots of a chunk belong together.
  • Timing across chunks. A crossing net has one slack and several pieces. The tree order from the driver (alpha) is a property of the whole net, so the coordinator decides the order of the chunks along the path and each chunk routes its piece from the upstream slot. Spacing is a property of a slot too: the next track at the edge stays empty.
  • Cells near the edge. Route-first moves cells. A cell stays in its chunk, so the rows of a chunk end at the edge and a slide or a push stops there. A cell whose best site is across the edge is the placement coordinator's business, not the router's.
  • Clock, power, macros. Clock leaf nets are local; trunks cross and go with the long wires. The power grid is fixed metal for every chunk. A macro inside a chunk is as today; a chunk edge along a macro edge wastes the channel, so edges should run in channels or through logic.
  • How small. The share of nets that cross grows as chunks shrink, and each crossing net costs coordinator work and a fixed slot. Somewhere the gain in parallel work is eaten by slots. That point is the number to find.

Experiments, cheapest first

  1. Count on what exists. Cut the routed eyeriss results by the five placement chunks and by grids from 2×2 to 32×32. Per cut: nets that cross, their share of wire and of the worst slack, feedthroughs, crossings per net, and crossings per edge and layer against the track supply. No code in the router. This says how small a chunk can be before most nets are long.
  2. Oracle slots. Split the placed DEF into chunk DEFs with a port at every place the flat route crosses an edge. rf_route already takes a port with metal on a track as a terminal, so each chunk routes with the existing binary, in parallel. Stitch, check and time the result. This is the best case of way 2: slots as good as a flat route would pick. Compare wire, Fmax, CPU and wall time with the flat run, and count the edge findings to size the contract.
  3. Estimated slots. The same with slots from the tile estimate instead of the flat route. The difference to experiment 2 is the price of choosing slots early, and the size of what way 3 has to recover.
  4. Long wires first, emulated. Route the crossing nets alone on the whole chip (--only), feed the result back as fixed wires, and route the chunks. A net with wires in the input is already treated as fixed metal. Compare with experiments 2 and 3 on the critical nets.
  5. Moving slots, only if experiment 3 loses clearly against experiment 2: one round of slot moves on the nets with the largest detour.

Stop rule: if oracle slots (experiment 2) already lose more than a few percent of Fmax or wire against the flat route at the chunk count that gives the wall time wanted, slots are the wrong cut for these designs and the long wires go first.

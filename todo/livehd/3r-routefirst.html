3r-routefirst Native routing with movable logic

Route and legalize in one loop, guided by routing instead of placement, and be better and faster than single-thread OpenROAD. The router is pass/route, run with lhd route. It routes every signal net on M2 to M7, moves the cells that block a pin, and with --set route.prelegal=true gives each cell its site when the first net that needs it is routed.

Cut back on 2026-11-25 to what is still to do. What was measured or tried and closed is in tools/routefirst/README.md (method, rule table, measurements, the steps after placement, pin density, chunk routing on NVDLA). The page before this cut is in the git history at commit 132ec87bc, the first plan (OpenROAD as data router) up to 4d31a8b7e.

Depends on

  • 2r-route supplies the pose before terminal legalization and the pin slacks (legroute_plan*/input.def, timing.tsv).
  • 2p-pcrs supplies the clock tree. Its cells are in the DEF; its wires are not (see Clock).
  • 2f-scalability supplies the chunks for routing by chunks.
  • 3s-postroute is the finishing pass after this router (free M2 and M3 for power and clock, fillers, antennas, hold). Not started.

Direction (user)

  • The flow must not call OpenROAD. OpenROAD is the referee only: design rules, extraction and timing of a finished result.
  • Flops may move during routing, with their clock leaves rerouted or the movement of clock cells limited.
  • The most critical or most complex nets choose first. Cells that do not fit move inside their tile, then to a nearby tile with the lowest demand.
  • Long, very critical nets may get extra spacing. The spare M2 and M3 go to the finishing pass.
  • 2026-11-25: areas routed at a very high pin density are very hard to close, so the clouds are to be thinned before routing. More wire is acceptable if the frequency holds. Pin density belongs as early in the flow as it has an effect.

Where it stands

  • Small designs (minimax, sha3, ternip; 15k to 21k nets). Every net routed, no rule or connectivity finding, 34 to 80 CPU-seconds against 1,247 to 2,179 for single-thread OpenROAD, and 9 to 28% higher Fmax. Not rerun since the clock default changed.
  • Eyeriss (1,419 µm die, 1,040 macros, 294k to 438k nets). Fully routed on the LiveHD frame and on the ORFS-placement frame, 15 to 51 times less CPU than OpenROAD. The ORFS netlist placed by LiveHD stops with 52 of 433,445 nets left.
  • NVDLA. M, A and P route completely and time above OpenROAD's route of the same placement (1,148 against 1,108 MHz, 1,307 against 1,269, 820 against 804). C routes completely without a time cap (280,621 nets, 2,820 CPU-seconds, 320.5 MHz against 264.1). O is not closed: 27 of 232,280 nets are open in the best run (route-first with route.spread=0.8 and route.pin_footprint=25, 461.9 MHz, 19% more wire), 51 on the legalized placement with synth.place.legalize.pin_footprint=20 (469.9 MHz, 6.5% more wire), 387 to 706 without either. OpenROAD routes O completely at 461.7 MHz. M, A, P and C were not rerun with the router of 2026-11-25.
  • Clock default. synth.place.pcrs.initial is false since 2026-11-25: no first clock tree, so sizing and buffer repair time an ideal clock. Eyeriss goes from 161 to 378 MHz routed, NVDLA O from 490 to 502.
  • Chunks. lhd route chunk routes grid homes in separate workers and lhd route repair closes what the assembly leaves. Complete on M, A and P at or near the flat frequency (−0.5% to +2%); the crossing nets still go to the coordinator.
  • Options that exist and are off by default: route.prelegal, route.spread, route.pin_footprint, route.pin_cost, route.space_frac, route.long_frac; synth.place.legalize.pin_footprint, pin_density, pin_padding_critical; synth.place.pcrs.leaf_pull.

Clock

PCRS builds and places the clock tree, and the DEF LiveHD writes after final legalization carries its clock nets tagged as clock but without wires. The router routes them itself, all of them before the first data net, with the ordinary rules.

Pins of one cell that share tracks

Not started. This is what is left on NVDLA O after the clouds were thinned, and it is a property of the cells, not of the placement.

The problem

  • The cell. AO222x2 has six inputs on M1 columns 54 nm apart. Four of them (C1, C2, B2, B1) are bars 94 nm tall on consecutive columns, so each crosses two or three M2 tracks, and the four share those tracks. A pad on one track blocks the same track for the neighbour column (the pads would end 26 nm apart), so the four pins need alternating tracks, and each pad then needs its own way up: the M3 pitch is 36 nm against 54 for the columns, so every second pin has no M3 track above it and must jog on M2 first. AO221x1, AO32x1, OR4x1 and OR5x1 have the same shape with fewer pins.
  • What happens. The pins of one cell are routed as parts of different nets, at different times. The first takes the best track, its wire runs over the neighbours, and wires that only pass take the M3 above the cell. In five failures looked at in detail, the M3 track above the failed pin carried another net and 83% to 100% of the M3 tracks over the cell were in use; in one, all three M2 tracks of the pin were taken, one by a pin of the same cell.
  • How much. In the best O run 16 of the 27 open nets end on an inner input of an AO222x2, most of them on select nets of 18 to 34 pins that visit one such cell after another, in bins of 19 pins per µm² at the median. On the unthinned placement 175 of 407 failed pins were AO222x2 inputs. The 25 walled-in pins of the eyeriss tail look the same and were not classified.
  • Why it is not solved by what exists. route.reserve gives the pins of one cell their pads together and checks that each pad can leave, but it holds only the pad, not the way up, and it runs after a first failure. Reserving the pads of 11,851 such cells before routing moved O from 387 to 354 open nets. Rip-up brings the same wires back. Mirroring the cell was tried and removed (no gain in three runs). OpenROAD routes these cells, with access patterns computed per cell before routing.

Ways to try

  1. Access patterns per master. For each master, orientation and phase of the cell against the M3 tracks (two phases for a 54 nm site), list the joint assignments of a track and a way up for all pins that are legal together. A cell then takes a whole pattern, chosen by what is free around it, before any of its nets is routed. The patterns are few and can be computed once per library.
  2. Hold the way up. Keep the pad, its via and a short piece of M3 (or the M2 jog to the next free column) as metal of the net, the way the landing in front of a macro pin is kept, and put it back after a rip-up.
  3. Order. The user's rule is that the most complex nets choose first. The pins of cells with few patterns are the complex part: route their escapes before the data nets, or route the nets that touch such cells first.
  4. Keep passing wires off the cell. route.pin_cost prices M2 and M3 by tile. Price the tracks over a cell whose pins are not out yet instead, and drop the price when they are.
  5. Select nets as trunks. A net with one pin on each of 30 such cells is best served by a trunk that passes each cell on the column the cell's pattern reserved for it, with a tap, instead of a tree grown pin by pin.
  6. Fewer such cells. Mapping chooses them. A cost for masters with four or more pins on consecutive columns, or a pin-dense variant of the dont-use list, trades area for access. Only if the router side does not close the gap.

Experiments, cheapest first

  1. Do patterns exist in place? For the cells of the 27 open nets of O, remove the M2 and M3 wires of other nets over the cell and count the joint assignments the existing reservation search finds. If there are none, the placement or the library is the limit and ways 1 to 5 cannot help.
  2. Oracle for the held way up. On the routed O result, reopen the nets around each failed cell with lhd route repair (pin_neighbors, reserve_first), route the cell's own nets first and count what closes. An earlier trial on 12 cells closed 8 of 20; repeat it on the thinned placement.
  3. Patterns before routing for the masters that fail most (AO222x2, AO221x1, AO32x1), with the way up held, on O. Measure open nets, wire and CPU against 27, 2,125 mm and 5,562 s.
  4. Mapping without AO222x2 on O, as the price of way 6: cells, area, frequency, open nets.

Done when O routes completely, at no more wire than the 19% the thinning costs today, and M, A, P, C and eyeriss do not get slower.

Routing by chunks

Asked by the user on 2026-11-24: many small chunks, each with its own routing, and the open question of the long wires that cross them. The grid implementation exists (lhd route chunk, lhd route repair, the driver reference) and its measurements on the five NVDLA partitions are in tools/routefirst/README.md. In short: interiors route in parallel workers, crossing nets and spills go to the coordinator, and M, A and P come out complete. At a two by two cut 2 to 5% of the nets cross and carry 13 to 41% of the wire; at 32 by 32 they carry 83 to 90%.

What chunks would buy

  • Wall time. rf_route is one thread. Eyeriss takes 26 to 100 minutes; chunks route in parallel.
  • The tail. Time grows faster than the net count because late nets search a full chip. A chunk bounds every search and every rip-up.
  • Memory per worker, and incremental work: a change inside a chunk reroutes that chunk if its boundary stays as it was.

What a boundary is for a track router

Wires run on tracks in one direction per layer. A wire can cross a vertical chunk edge only on a horizontal layer (M2, M4, M6) and a horizontal edge only on M3, M5 or M7. A boundary pin is therefore a slot: an edge, a layer and a track. If the chunks tile the die, the two sides of an edge share the slot and there is no top-level wire at all: a net that crosses is two half nets that end on the same slot. A net that passes through a chunk without a pin in it is a feedthrough, a two-slot net of that chunk like any other. Supply is not the limit: a vertical edge has about 26 M2, 21 M4 and 16 M6 tracks per micron.

The chunks must also agree on the rules at the edge, because the limits reach across it: 31 to 48 nm end to end on one track, facing ends on the next track, 34 nm between cuts. Either a contract (no wire end and no cut within a margin of the edge except at a slot) or an order (neighbours route one after the other and see each other's edge wires) is needed. The contract keeps chunks independent. The order needs four colours of chunks and makes a reroute depend on the neighbours.

Three ways to handle the long wires

  1. Long wires first. Route every net that crosses a chunk edge on the whole chip, freeze it, and hand each chunk those wires as fixed metal. The long nets get straight tracks and the best layers, and they are the critical ones: on eyeriss the worst paths are nets of millimetres. But this part is serial and sees the whole chip, a crossing net still ends at cell pins inside chunks, and a change to one such net touches several chunks. It is the "longest first" order under another name, and the flat test of that order (item 5 above) is its first data point.
  2. Slots first, chunks independent. The coordinator gives every crossing net its slots from the tile estimate, then each chunk routes alone and respects them. This scales, runs in parallel and keeps a reroute local. The cost is that a slot is chosen before any wire exists: the route that the flat router would take may want another track, another layer or another place along the edge, and the detour is paid on both sides.
  3. Slots that move. Start as in 2. Each chunk reports, for each slot, what moving it would save: tracks along the edge, or a layer. The coordinator moves a slot when the two sides together gain, and only the two half nets are rerouted. A net that failed in a chunk for lack of a way to its slot asks first. One or two rounds, critical nets first, which is the route-first idea applied to slots instead of sites.

My expectation: 2 is the base because it is the only one that scales, 3 recovers most of what 2 loses, and 1 stays for the few nets where a slot is the wrong abstraction: the clock trunks and the handful of unbuffered nets that cross the die. A mixed rule (the worst share by slack and span goes first on the upper layers, the rest gets slots) costs little serial time if the share is a few percent.

What needs care

  • One tree per net. The coordinator fixes which edges a net crosses, as a tree over the chunks. Each chunk then joins its own pins and slots of that net. Two crossings of one net between the same two chunks would make a loop, and a chunk that connects its slots but not to each other would leave the net open, so the tree must say which slots of a chunk belong together.
  • Timing across chunks. A crossing net has one slack and several pieces. The tree order from the driver (alpha) is a property of the whole net, so the coordinator decides the order of the chunks along the path and each chunk routes its piece from the upstream slot. Spacing is a property of a slot too: the next track at the edge stays empty.
  • Cells near the edge. Route-first moves cells. A cell stays in its chunk, so the rows of a chunk end at the edge and a slide or a push stops there. A cell whose best site is across the edge is the placement coordinator's business, not the router's.
  • Clock, power, macros. Clock leaf nets are local; trunks cross and go with the long wires. The power grid is fixed metal for every chunk. A macro inside a chunk is as today; a chunk edge along a macro edge wastes the channel, so edges should run in channels or through logic.
  • How small. The share of nets that cross grows as chunks shrink, and each crossing net costs coordinator work and a fixed slot. Somewhere the gain in parallel work is eaten by slots. That point is the number to find.

Still to do for chunks

  • Crossing nets in the workers. Production chunking gives every crossing net to the coordinator. The boundary-slot trial (two-terminal nets, eight per partition) accepted 1 to 6 of 8 with estimated slots, 3 to 7 after moving failed slots with neighbour feedback, and 7 or 8 with the slot a flat route would use. So slots work when they are good, and moving them recovers most of the loss. Extend it to all crossing nets and to trees with more than two terminals.
  • Memory. Every worker still loads the whole frame. A worker should get its chunk and the fixed metal near it.
  • How small. With the same total budget four homes were best on C and two to four on O; more homes need more total CPU. Find the size where the coordinator's share stops the gain, on eyeriss as well.
  • Chunk pins after legalization. Region boundary pins are refined once, before sizing, and nothing looks at them after the final legalization; on eyeriss 447 of 738 bus groups had lost their order. Run the boundary observation on the final placement and compare crossing order and per-edge demand with the refined ones before any chunked route relies on them.
  • Stop rule for slots: if slots as good as a flat route would pick already lose more than a few percent of Fmax or wire at the chunk count that gives the wall time wanted, the long wires go first instead.

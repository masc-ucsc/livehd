2p-pcrs PCRS clock: a placement-aware clock network owned by LiveHD

Plan written 2026-11-19 from a question-and-answer session. Nothing below is started; only the reset part of pass/place/pcrs exists. This is a high-level plan, not an execution plan. Names of options, steps and files are proposals. The section Hints from the post-route runs was added the same day from the HighTide post-route session of 2026-11-18 and 19.

LiveHD places and times a design on ideal clocks, and OpenROAD builds the clock tree afterwards on a placement that never looked at the clock. This page plans the clock part of PCRS (power, clock, reset, scan): LiveHD pulls the sinks of a clock together while it places them, forms small and uniform leaf groups, decides where the clock is gated, schedules the arrival of every group, builds the network above the groups, and times setup and hold on that network. OpenROAD no longer synthesizes a clock tree. It routes the one LiveHD built. What the clock steps cannot fix at a reasonable cost is reported against the RTL, because changing the RTL is an accepted answer.

Why

  • The backend stops in the clock stage. In the HighTide asap7 cohort, two of the three LiveHD failures that reached the backend stopped in OpenROAD's CTS and hold repair, before any signal was routed (2r-route).
  • Hold is paid after the fact. OpenROAD's hold repair added 82,892 buffers on gemmini (utilization 49.1% → 53.2%) and reached its buffer limit on NVDLA/partition_a with hold WNS −136 ps left. LiveHD only reserves room for those buffers today (2r decision C1). It does not know where they go or why.
  • The clock does not pull anything. Clock nets are excluded from placement (prects_networks), so flops are placed by their data nets only, and a clock gate is held by its enable net alone. A gate can sit far from the flops it drives.
  • Setup is optimized on a clock that will not exist. Sizing, repair and resynthesis see zero skew. The real tree then moves every endpoint by its own latency, and the critical paths after CTS are not the ones LiveHD worked on.
  • Most clock power is at the leaves. About 80% of clock capacitance is at the leaf level (Cheon et al., cited by Deng et al.); on the ISPD 2010 clock benchmarks 58% of it is buffer capacitance. The leaf level is decided by where the flops are, which is a placement decision.

K1Leaf groupsSinks of the same clock are placed close to each other and grouped into leaf groups of 6 to 30 flops behind one driver (a buffer or a clock gate). The goal is a homogeneous, predictable tree. Getting the clock gates right matters more than the last picosecond of skew.

SourceWhat it showsWhat this plan takes
Mangiras et al., Soft-Clustering Driven Flip-flop Placement Targeting Clock-induced OCV, ISPD 2020 (soft_clustering.pdf) Before CTS, flops and gates move toward the weighted mean of nearby cluster centres. Membership is soft (k-harmonic means) and mixes distance with the position of timing neighbours: cells on the same clock net that launch and capture the same path, weighted by slack (a = 0.35 distance, 0.65 neighbours). It works bottom-up over the gating hierarchy, moves only cells with positive slack inside their timing-feasible region, and avoids dense or congested areas. Six industrial designs (82k to 1.5M cells, 14–28 nm): setup TNS −42%, WNS −28%, hold WNS −45%, hold TNS −73% on average, for 0.4–8% of flow time. Plain distance-only flop clumping made timing worse on several designs. The membership function becomes the force of step E. Its limit: it only guides someone else's CTS through placement. Here the groups become the real leaves.
Deng, Cai, Zhou, Register Clustering Methodology for Low Power Clock Tree Synthesis, JCST 2015 (clustering.pdf) Hard clustering of registers on a fixed placement, one local buffer per cluster, and the buffers become the sinks of any CTS. Three algorithms; the greedy one (GSR) adds a register to the nearest cluster within a maximum radius, load and fanout, else opens a new cluster. The buffer of a cluster is the smallest one that meets slew at the farthest register. ISPD 2009/2010 benchmarks: clock power −31%, skew −4%, latency −5%, CTS run time about 10× lower, because the tree above has far fewer sinks. The hard grouping of step D (radius, load and fanout limits; smallest driver that meets slew), and the rule that the tree above only sees leaf drivers.
Khan, Clock Mesh Analysis and Optimization using Surrogate Models with Graph Neural Networks, PhD thesis, UCSC 2026 (phd_thesis.pdf) A mesh is robust to variation but STA cannot time it (loops, several drivers), and the first-moment method is 74 ps off in delay and 27.5 ps in skew under variation. A GNN surrogate on asap7 predicts delay, slew and skew within 3.3–4.3 ps of SPICE and power within 3.4%, 18× to 1706× faster than the first-moment method. Used in a genetic search over mesh buffer sizes it improves 99th-percentile skew by 49.1%, power by 25.7% and buffer area by 37.3% over MeshWorks, with 6.8% more slew. The top-level tree enters the model only as an input delay and slew per mesh buffer. Its future work names local clock buffers with small local trees below the mesh, where "the inputs of these buffers take the place of the flip-flop pins". The mesh top of step H, timed by the surrogate. The leaf interface of this plan is the thesis's local-buffer extension. Its metric list (global and local skew, slew, power, robustness, routing resources) is the report of step A.
Li et al., iCTS: Iterative and Hierarchical Clock Tree Synthesis With Skew-Latency-Load Tree, TCAD 2025 (abstract), and its DAC 2024 version (full text). Open source in iEDA. Level by level, bottom-up: cluster, build topology, buffer. Clusters are balanced (k-means with a min-cost flow, then annealing) on a cost that is the variance of net capacitance and net delay. The topology is a bounded-skew tree, shortened by a shallow-light step, then made skew-legal again. Buffer delay is a first-order fit in input slew and load; a node gets a lower bound of its insertion delay before its buffer exists, so later merges disturb less. On 28 nm, 1.2k–27k flops, OpenROAD's tree has 1.4–1.5× the latency, 1.7–2.2× the skew and 1.2–1.3× the buffers of iCTS, and breaks the 80 ps skew bound on two designs. It does not beat the commercial tool on every design. The model for the tree top of step G. The variance cost is the homogeneity of K1 written as an objective. The cheap buffer model and the insertion-delay bound keep the timing engine out of the inner loop.
Li et al., A Method for Synthesizing Ultra-Large-Scale Clock Trees (IB-KSR), Algorithms 2025 (full text) IB-KSR is "incomplete balanced KSR", where KSR is the K-splitting clustering of Deng et al. above. It replaces the complete graph by the k nearest neighbours of each register, runs Kruskal on those edges, cuts long edges, and splits any tree above the fanout limit at the edge that halves it. Time goes from quadratic to O(k·n·log²n). 114k–185k registers. The evidence is weak: no delay model is described, the baseline is the authors' own GSR, and it uses 21% more buffers and 87× the run time of that GSR. Only the scaling rule for steps D and G: neighbours from a sparse graph, and a balanced split to enforce a hard fanout limit.
Ji et al., DiffCCD: Differentiable Concurrent Clock and Data Optimization, ICCAD 2025 (full text) After CTS, cell positions (within a small radius) and clock buffer sizes are optimized together by gradient on a differentiable timer, on a GPU. Against OpenROAD's repair_timing after its CTS: WNS 22% and TNS 45% better, 9.4× faster, on 20k–798k cells. Setup only: no hold, no gate sizing, no post-route numbers. Step F: clock arrival and data path are decided on the same slack, in the placement timing loop. The gap it leaves is hold, which K7 puts in the schedule.
Choi et al., Multisource Clock Tree Synthesis Through Sink Clustering and Fast Clock Latency Prediction, ISCAS 2023 (abstract only) Local trees under tap drivers fed by an H-tree. Sinks are clustered by weight-balanced k-means so the tap loads are equal, and the number of trees is searched with a learned latency predictor instead of running CTS. Latency −11.8%, synthesis time −64% on a few circuits. The same structure as the mesh or spine top of step H: balanced tap loads, and a fast predictor to choose the number of taps.
Cheng et al., Cross-Mesh Clock Network Synthesis, Electronics 2023 (full text) One horizontal and one vertical mesh line through the core centre. Each register starts with its own clock gate; neighbouring clusters merge while the load stays under 60–90% of the gate's limit (26–38 registers per gate at 90%). Dummy loads balance the branches. Against a uniform mesh: capacitance −29%, skew −80%. Benchmarks are tiny (at most 1,728 registers, 90 nm) and there is no tree baseline. A gate per leaf group, merged up to a load limit, lands in the same 6–30 range as K1. The cross is the cheapest non-tree top and a candidate for the routable spine of the open point on mesh wires.
Kwon and Park, Shallow Clock Tree Pre-Estimation for Designing Clock Tree Synthesizable Verilog RTLs, Electronics 2023 (full text) A netlist-only estimate: no placement, no wires, delay is the sum of cell delays on each clock path, and paths are padded with buffers to the longest one. Not a CTS method. Nothing for the network. Only the idea behind the synthesis level of the clock report (K9): clock structure that will hurt is visible before placement, while changing the RTL is still cheap.
Kahng, Kundu, Thumathy, Scalable Flip-Flop Clustering Using Divide and Conquer for Capacitated K-Means, GLSVLSI 2024 (found by the literature check) Capacitated k-means for flop trays, split into small min-cost-flow problems: 100× faster than the earlier flow for 1.1% power. asap7, up to 64k flops. It is the basis of OpenROAD's cluster_flops. The reference for the size-capped hard assignment of step D, and for multi-bit banking if it is ever planned.

OpenROAD is moving too

The literature check (2026-11-19, OpenROAD master) found that the baseline is no longer the plain H-tree of the cohort runs. The items marked "reported" come from the check and are not yet reproduced here (F9).

  • Leaf clusters. -sink_clustering_enable builds one leaf level below the H-tree. Without explicit limits a cluster grows to a capacitance limit, with at most 30 sinks (reported). That is the upper end of K1.
  • Useful skew. clock_tree_synthesis -useful_skew, merged 2026-10-29 and off by default, inserts delay buffers to reach per-register targets from a skew budget.
  • Clock estimate in placement. global_placement -virtual_cts gives each sink a latency proportional to its path in a spanning tree (reported, merged 2026-06).
  • Gate cloning. A gate whose flops do not fit one cluster is cloned per part (reported, a commit of 2026-11-13). An open pull request makes power-driven placement pull leaf clusters together.
  • Still missing there. No incremental CTS, no skew groups, no mesh, and the known weak points remain: deep buffer chains, latency, and skew with many macros.

So single features are not the advantage. What LiveHD can have and a staged flow cannot: one timer and one set of decisions for placement, gating level and arrival schedule; groups that are uniform because placement made them so; the gating level decided with the enable function in hand; a report against the RTL; and a local rebuild after a small change.

Design

The clock as PCRS sees it

  • Clock domain: one SDC clock root and everything it reaches through buffers, inverters and gates. Domains are found as the timing engine finds them today.
  • Sink: a flop clock pin, a memory or macro clock pin (with its Liberty insertion delay), a latch, or a clock leaving through a port.
  • Gating function: (domain, enable expression). Every sink has zero or more of them, taken from what synthesis inferred. The cells that implement a function are PCRS's to choose (K3).
  • Timing neighbours: two sinks of the same domain joined by a data path, with the slack of that path (the soft-clustering paper's definition).
  • Leaf group: 6–30 sinks with the same gating function, one driver, one scheduled arrival.
  • Leaf interface: what a top network sees of a group: driver position, input capacitance, wanted arrival and tolerance. Trees and meshes are built against this interface only (K6).

Soft clustering in global placement (K4)

  • Each sink is attracted to the nearby cluster centres of its own gating function. The weight mixes distance with where its timing neighbours are, as in the paper. Cluster centres are a by-product of the previous iteration, so the force costs little.
  • The force grows with density overflow going down, like the other late forces, so it shapes the placement without fighting spreading.
  • A cell on a critical path is pulled less. A bin that is already dense in cells or pins takes no more flops.
  • Useful skew changes the paper's rule. Two flops on a critical path that need different arrivals must not share a group. They go to sibling groups under a common parent, which still gives them a long shared clock path. Wanted arrival is therefore one more coordinate of membership.

Hard leaf groups (K1)

  • Formed once on the converged placement, in the style of GSR: a group takes the nearest sink while it stays inside a maximum radius, load and fanout, and its sinks agree on gating function and wanted arrival.
  • Homogeneous means: few driver sizes, a narrow band of load and wire radius per group, so the delay of a leaf stage is nearly the same everywhere. The top network then sees uniform sinks, and an arrival offset is a deliberate, discrete step. A refinement pass moves border sinks between groups to lower the variance of group capacitance and delay (the iCTS clustering cost).
  • 30 is the fanout and slew limit of one driver. 6 is where a dedicated driver or gate stops paying for itself. Sinks left in a group below 6 join a neighbour or lose their gate (next section).
  • Memories and macros are groups of one with their own insertion delay.

Gating level (K3)

  • Clone: one gate cell per leaf group. The gate is the leaf driver, so a gated group costs no extra buffer.
  • Merge: groups with the same function that end up adjacent share one gate.
  • Raise: when every group below a tree node shares a function, or a common factor of their enables (the chained form synthesis already produces), the gate moves to that node. More of the tree stops toggling, but the enable must arrive earlier by the latency below the gate.
  • Lower or drop: a function left with too few flops goes back to an enable on the data path, and its flops join an ungated neighbour group.
  • The choice is a cost: switched clock capacitance (with the enable's activity), cell area, and the slack of the enable's clock-gating check at that level. Structural changes happen between timing rounds.
  • Each flop keeps its (clock, enable) behaviour, so lhd lec compares the same function. Clock buffers and inverter pairs are identities.

Arrival schedule (K5)

  • One wanted arrival per leaf group, not per flop. It comes from the setup slack before and after the group's flops and from their hold slack, on the engine's criticality window.
  • The offset is bounded: by hold at the derated margin, by a maximum range (a fraction of the period), and by what the network can realize in a few uniform steps.
  • Realized in two places: the leaf driver (size, or one of a few delay steps) and, for a tree, the branches above. A mesh delivers one arrival, so below a mesh the leaf is the only place.
  • The schedule is recomputed at every timing round together with sizing and repair, so data-path optimization and clock arrival are decided on the same slack (the DiffCCD direction).

Top network (K6)

  • Tree. Built bottom-up and hierarchically over the leaf interfaces to the wanted arrivals: nearby nodes merge under limits on skew, latency and load; a buffer is chosen with a cheap load-based delay model; the timing engine checks each level. Registers that talk to each other merge early, so their shared path is long (K8). Neighbour search uses a sparse graph, never all pairs.
  • Mesh. A grid over the region of a domain, mesh buffers on it, a short stub from the grid to every leaf driver, and a tree from the root to the mesh buffers. Buffer sizes come from the thesis's search, with its surrogate predicting arrival, slew and power at the leaf drivers. Those arrivals enter the timing engine as the clock arrival of each leaf driver.
  • Which one. Per domain, or per region of a domain. A mesh is chosen where the tree cannot meet the skew target under the derate of K8 and the domain is dense enough to pay for the wire. Both report the same metrics.

Timing on the built clock (K8)

  • The engine propagates the clock through the PCRS network and gives every sink an early and a late arrival. Setup and hold use them instead of zero.
  • Derate on clock cells and wires; the shared part of the launch and capture paths is not derated twice. A longer shared path is then worth picoseconds in LiveHD's own slack, which is what makes the clustering decisions measurable.
  • Before the network exists (early placement stages) the engine uses an estimate: ideal clock plus the scheduled arrival of the group a sink would join.

Hold (K7)

  • The schedule never creates a hold violation at the model's margin.
  • Cheap fixes before hand-off, in order: shift the schedule, change a cell (flop or driver) on the short path, add a delay cell where setup slack covers it. A budget on added area stops the pass.
  • What is left goes to the post-route pass on extracted RC. It runs in OpenROAD first, and natively when a post-route timing loop exists.
  • Every hold fix is placed by LiveHD, so its sites are known before the routing estimate.

Clock report: what to ask the RTL for (K9)

Every clock step knows when it gave up or paid too much. The report collects those cases instead of hiding them in a worse tree. An item names the source location (through the source map), the cost in the unit that hurts (slack, added cells, switched clock capacitance, hold buffers) and the change that would remove it. Items are ranked by cost, and an item appears only when PCRS could not fix it or the fix cost more than a threshold. The report is machine-readable, so an agent can act on it.

The synthesis level needs no placement and runs in seconds, so it is useful long before a clock network exists. Later levels repeat the check with real positions and real arrivals, and drop the items that placement solved.

Incremental

  • A group whose sinks, positions and schedule did not change keeps its driver, name and place. A change rebuilds the groups it touches and the path from them to the root.
  • With chunks (2f-scalability): leaf groups never cross a chunk, each chunk has a clock entry with an arrival as a boundary time, and the top network connects chunk entries.

  • H1 — one owner of the clock cone. Three placement passes edited clock cells before they were stopped one by one. IO resynthesis remapped a clock gate into a buffer, a NAND and an inverter; OpenROAD's CTS does not treat the AND function behind that inverter as a clock root, so 8,357 flops of coralnpu got no tree (fixed by c3c0930c8). Repair protected clock and reset buffers by instance name, and unnamed wiring nodes all shared the empty name, so unrelated data buffers were protected too (fixed by 230eead8f). Buffer cleanup now rewrites gate chains. PCRS should publish one clock-cone classification that every pass asks, instead of each pass walking back from clock pins on its own. This widens the "two owners" risk below to every pass that edits cells.
  • H2 — gate depth is skew between groups. With cascaded gates the gated flops sit two cells below the root while an ungated domain (the reset synchronizer) sits at the root. Merging the cascade into one AND3 halved coralnpu's hold TNS before repair (−295k to −145k ps) but left most of the endpoints: the remaining violators are still registers of the gated domain. This is a direct case for K3 and K5 together: the arrival of a gated group has to be scheduled against the ungated groups it talks to. It is also a ready-made test for step D.
  • H3 — macro clock pins are the first useful-skew target. litepci's worst hold is at fakeram address pins in both arms, from the macro's hold requirement plus the clock latency to the macro, and both arms pay hours of buffer insertion for it. A macro is a leaf group of one (K1), so its arrival can be scheduled directly (K5). Moving that one arrival is cheaper than delaying hundreds of address and data paths. Step F should take litepci as its first case.
  • H4 — hold fixes need room where the short paths are. On vortex the hold buffers that failed to legalize sat in the strip between a pin-dense macro face and the core edge, and along the IO edge where LiveHD had about 12k cells within 15 µm against about 1.4k in OpenROAD's placement. LiveHD now keeps OpenROAD's macro halo and a floor on macro channels, which fixed litepci and cnn. For K7 this means the bounded pre-route fix must place its cells with the same rules, and the reserve of 2r has to sit near macro pin faces and IO registers, not be spread evenly.
  • H5 — ideal-clock slack cannot rank clock changes. NVDLA/partition_c's pre-CTS setup WNS moved from −130 to −1,799 ps on a change that touched no data path, and the gate flatten looked worse before CTS (−130 to −615 ps) but better after global routing (−520 against −555 ps). Until step B exists, a clock step can only be judged on the routed result, on same-binary pairs, and a pair should be run twice.
  • H6 — the control arm is already scripted. postroute.py runs the reference and a LiveHD placement through the same deployed OpenROAD stages and keeps every stage log. It records setup slack per stage, routed wirelength and DRC. Hold endpoints and hold buffers per stage were read from the logs by hand in these runs; adding them to its metrics gives step A its report. Its LiveHD arm imports a DEF onto OpenROAD's floorplan, which is also where a "no CTS" variant (step C) would start.
  • H7 — leaf limits need slew, not only fanout. The reset part of PCRS limited a level by fanout 16 and left pipeline flops driving reset pins at 5 to 6.5 ns of slew on litepci until its cells were sized against the Liberty transition limit (acac8ac6c). The timing engine now propagates slew to each sink through one pole per wire (e9e759f9d), so the "smallest driver that meets slew at the farthest sink" rule of K1 can use it directly.
  • H8 — order with 2w-wlresynth. That task may duplicate flops per load cluster. Each copy is a new clock sink at a new place, so hard leaf groups must be formed after it, and the soft force should treat the copies as separate sinks.
  • H9 — warm runs. A warm rerun restores the placement only when the compiled design and every placement decision are byte-identical. One unordered container in the frontend was enough to re-place vortex for 25 minutes (e02d898f4). Group formation, gate cloning and the schedule need stable orders (position ties broken by name) and a cache key that includes their options.

Facts to confirm first

  • F1 — no-CTS recipe. What OpenROAD needs to accept a clock network it did not build: clock net type and routing rules, propagated clocks in the SDC, protection of clock cells from its own repair, and whether its timing repair still edits clock nets. HighTide's workaround table (CLAUDE.md) already documents three behaviours that matter here: CTS skips a buffer whose source type is TIMING as an existing clock buffer (CTS-0105), CTS runs its own detailed placement that ignores the flow's placement arguments (DPL-0036), and SKIP_CTS_REPAIR_TIMING and SKIP_INCREMENTAL_REPAIR switch off the repair passes that would otherwise edit a network LiveHD built.
  • F2 — clock cells. Per platform (asap7, sky130, nangate45): buffer, inverter, gate and delay cell families the LEF can place, and the insertion delay of memory clock pins.
  • F3 — sign-off variation. Which derate and pessimism removal the ORFS sign-off uses on the cohort. If none, K8 needs a declared derate applied to both arms.
  • F4 — activity. Whether enable activity is available (from lhd sim) for the gating cost, or a default probability is used.
  • F5 — cohort clock structure. Per design: sinks per domain, flops per gating function, gate depth, and the share of flops whose function has fewer than 6 or more than 30 flops.
  • F6 — thesis artifacts. Code, weights, technology coverage and run-time dependencies of the surrogate and of the mesh generator, and their licences.
  • F7 — mesh hand-off. How a mesh is written so OpenROAD keeps it and connects the stubs, and how the sign-off timer receives mesh arrivals.
  • F8 — reading OpenROAD's tree. The DEF reader rejects the non-default routing rules that OpenROAD CTS writes (2r). Step B needs to load those results.
  • F9 — the OpenROAD of the control arm. Which OpenROAD version the cohort recipes use, and whether it has sink clustering, -useful_skew, -virtual_cts and gate cloning. Reproduce each reported feature before it is used as a baseline. HighTide pins OpenROAD b65c274c with OpenSTA 8572175a (its 2026-07 validation), which is older than the -useful_skew merge and the gate-cloning commit quoted above, so the stronger control of step A needs a newer OpenROAD built next to the pinned one.

Steps

K5 and K6 put useful skew, tree and mesh in the first milestone. The order below is the order of dependency.

  • A — Controls. On the cohort, measure OpenROAD's CTS on today's LiveHD placements: clock buffers, clock capacitance, latency, global and local skew, sinks and radius per leaf buffer, hold buffers and where they land, and the stage each run reaches. Two controls: the recipe as it is today, and the best OpenROAD can do with its own clock features on (F9). These are the control arms of every later comparison.
  • B — Clock in the timing engine. Propagated arrivals, derate, common-path credit, gating checks and hold with real latency. Checked by loading an OpenROAD post-CTS result and comparing per-sink latency and endpoint slack with OpenSTA.
  • C — Hand-off without OpenROAD CTS. The simplest network (hard groups on a finished placement, a plain balanced tree) taken through routing with CTS skipped. It proves the recipe of F1 and the reports. No QoR claim.
  • D — Leaf groups and gates. Hard grouping with the homogeneity rules; gates as functions; clone and merge first, then raise, lower and drop with the cost model.
  • E — Soft clustering in global placement. The force, the area reservation, and the density and pin-density guards.
  • F — Arrival schedule. Wanted arrival per group, its bounds, its role in group membership, and its place in the timing rounds.
  • G — Tree top. The hierarchical builder to the scheduled arrivals, buffer placement, scaling to the largest domain of the cohort.
  • H — Mesh top. Mesh generation, the surrogate, buffer sizing, the selection rule, and the hand-off of F7.
  • I — Hold. Schedule bounds, the bounded pre-route fixes, and the post-route pass.
  • J — Incremental and chunks. Local rebuild after a small change, and the chunk clock entry.
  • L — Clock report (no step K, to keep it apart from the decisions). The synthesis level comes with step A, from the structure census of F5. Steps D, F, G, H and I each add their items when they land. Checked by applying a suggested change to one cohort design and measuring that the reported cost goes away.

Acceptance

Routed outcome in OpenROAD with the same die, density and constraints in both arms, as in 2r decision R6. Control: LiveHD placement, OpenROAD CTS, in the two variants of step A. New: LiveHD clock network, OpenROAD routing only. A win counts only against the stronger control.

  • No design stops at an earlier stage than its control, and the CTS and hold-repair failures of the cohort are gone.
  • Post-route setup and hold WNS and TNS with propagated clocks on extracted RC, under the variation setup of F3.
  • Hold buffers added after hand-off, as a count and as area. This is the number the cohort complained about.
  • Clock network: cells, capacitance (power), wirelength, maximum latency, global skew, local skew on the near-critical register pairs, and the spread of leaf-stage delay (the homogeneity of K1).
  • Routability and signal wirelength not worse; final DRC.
  • Wall time of the clock steps, and of an incremental rebuild after a one-module change.
  • LiveHD's own post-placement slack against the routed slack, per endpoint: the reason to own the clock is that the two agree.
  • The clock report of each design: its top items explain the clock cost that is left, and at least one suggested RTL change is shown to remove the cost it reported.

Risks

  • Pin density in flop groups. On litepci the detailed-route hotspots were a flop-array cluster with more than 9 pins/µm² (2026-11-16). Pulling flops together makes this worse unless the group radius respects the router's pin capacity.
  • Data timing and wirelength. Every move toward a clock group lengthens some data net. The force must lose against a critical data path.
  • Useful skew and hold. Scheduled offsets create short-path risk that the pre-route model sees only approximately. A schedule that is too aggressive moves the 82,892-buffer problem instead of removing it.
  • Model against routed clock. LiveHD times its tree on estimated wires; OpenROAD routes them. If the two disagree, the schedule is built on the wrong arrivals. Step C measures this gap before any optimization depends on it.
  • Mesh outside the comfort zone. A mesh needs wires LiveHD does not draw, a timer STA does not have, and a learned model in the flow. The last learned model in LiveHD (URoute) was deleted.
  • Two owners of clock gating. Synthesis infers gates and PCRS re-decides them. The split must be explicit, or the same gate is created and dropped at every round.
  • Passes that edit the clock cone. Resynthesis, buffer cleanup and repair each changed clock cells in the 2026-11 runs (H1). Any pass added later can do it again unless the cone is a shared, enforced classification.
  • Hold buffers without room. A correct schedule still leaves buffers to place, and they concentrate at macro pin faces and IO registers (H4), where rows are cut and pins are dense.
  • Too much in one milestone. Useful skew, tree and mesh together make a result hard to attribute. Each must be switchable so a comparison can turn one off.

Open points

  • Mesh wires: K2 says OpenROAD routes the clock, but a detailed router does not build a mesh. Either LiveHD writes the grid as fixed wires, or a spine that OpenROAD can route is the first non-tree network.
  • Where the surrogate runs: as a separate process, or as native inference inside lhd.
  • Multi-bit flops: a leaf group is a natural bank. Not planned until a platform with such cells is in the cohort.
  • Divided and generated clocks, clock muxes, and several clocks reaching one sink.
  • The reset tree of PCRS under useful skew: its flops are clock sinks like any other.
  • The power and scan parts of PCRS are not in this page.
